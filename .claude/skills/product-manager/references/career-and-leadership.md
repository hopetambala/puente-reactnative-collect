# Career and Leadership

For scope changes, leadership transitions, and interviews. Coaching, not generic leadership advice — every framework here names a specific failure mode and what it costs.

---

## Altitude and Horizon: the PM → Director model

Two axes explain most of what changes.

**Altitude (scope)**
- *PM:* close to the ground — customer problems, individual features, sprint priorities, one team's dynamics
- *Director:* the portfolio, cross-functional systems, org dynamics, budget allocation, market positioning

**Horizon (time)**
- *PM:* days, weeks, sprints; a quarter at most
- *Director:* a quarter is the *starting point*; annual planning, multi-year strategy, market shifts

### The waiter vs. the restaurant operator

| | PM (waiter) | Director (operator) |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | The individual diner | The whole system — staffing, margins, menu, suppliers |
| Authority | Influence without control | Portfolio decisions, budget, resourcing |
| Success metric | Table seven is happy | The restaurant is profitable, consistent, scalable |
| Relationship to customers | Direct, daily, intimate | Aggregate patterns, buyer personas, cohorts |
| Failure mode | Ignoring table seven | Obsessing over table seven's lemons |

The shift isn't losing empathy for customers — it's zooming out to see the restaurant rather than one table.

### Four transition zones

1. **Thinking altitude** — stop solving individual customer problems; start designing systems and teams that solve *classes* of problems.
2. **Persona shift** — stop obsessing over individual user personas; start thinking in buyer personas, market cohorts, and executive dynamics.
3. **Hero syndrome recovery** — stop being the person who saves the day; start getting satisfaction from team success. *Your product is your people, not the roadmap.*
4. **Direction creation** — stop waiting for clarity from above; start cascading context downward even when the inputs are incomplete.

Most people struggle with one or two zones more than the rest. Diagnosing which one is the leverage point.

### Named failure modes

**Hero syndrome** — *Looks like:* jumping in to solve directly, staying tactical, wanting visibility on individual wins. *Why:* PMs are trained to be helpful, and directors get fewer pats on the back, so people regress to the old reward loop. *Cost:* you under-perform as a director while over-functioning as a senior IC, and your team doesn't develop because you're in their way.

**Allergic to process** — *Looks like:* resisting shared structure, letting strong PMs run entirely independent playbooks. *Why:* early permissiveness feels like trust. *Cost:* stakeholders can't synthesize inconsistent outputs across teams.

**People-pleaser leadership** — *Looks like:* wanting to be liked, avoiding hard feedback, saying yes to preserve relationships. *Why:* the listening and empathy that made you a great PM become liabilities at organizational scale. *Cost:* you confuse popular with effective. Respect comes from clarity and hard calls, not niceness.

**Instant gratification trap** — *Looks like:* books, certifications, "what do I need to do to get promoted?" *Cost:* you can study your way to fluency in the vocabulary but not to readiness for the role. Director readiness requires war stories and lived humility.

**Black-and-white thinking** — *Looks like:* "this is an obvious decision," "why can't we fund both?", "why is everything so political here?" *Cost:* fast decisions at low confidence create downstream chaos. The grayscale isn't a leadership failure — it's the actual terrain.

### The Cascading Context Map

When organizational direction is vague, directors don't wait — they cascade:

1. Listen to top-level strategy (QBRs, company messaging, exec comms)
2. Extract the 3–5 priorities leadership actually stated — not twenty bullets
3. Map layer two: *how does our business unit accomplish these?*
4. Map layer three: *how does our product portfolio accomplish that?*
5. Map layer four: *what are my team's specific accountabilities that drive layer three?*
6. Communicate the cascade — not just what to do, but why it connects upward

**What it fixes:** teams wandering in the wilderness, shipping work that doesn't connect to strategy because nobody translated the context for them.

**The principle:** waiting for perfect clarity is a PM habit. Creating imperfect-but-useful clarity is a director skill.

**Pitfalls:** altitude theater (using strategic vocabulary while still doing PM work); a one-and-done context cascade (it's a recurring practice, not a kickoff deck); confusing kindness with leadership; premature director thinking as a PM, which reads as disengagement from the actual job.

## Director → VP / CPO

### The Three Ps

At this level accountability expands across three dimensions at once:

- **Product** — portfolio decisions, roadmap strategy, family coherence
- **Practice** — how the work gets done; process discipline, execution consistency, operating rhythms
- **People** — the dominant focus: org structure, talent matching, developing leaders, setting expectations *and inspecting them*

Most directors are strong on Product and adequate on Practice. **People — not managing individuals, but stewarding the organizational system — is where this transition breaks down.**

### The empowerment myth

The common false belief: *"once I get there, I'll finally be empowered to do what I always knew needed doing."*

Constraints don't disappear; they change shape. A PM's Rubik's Cube is 3×3. A director's is 5×5. A VP's is 7×7. A CPO's is 9×9. Same principles — competing priorities, incomplete information, stakeholder dynamics — with an exponentially larger blast radius per decision. The leaders who thrive made peace with this: you're not escaping constraints, you're developing capacity for larger ones.

### The VP → CPO paradigm shift

**VP mindset:** "What are we releasing? How does the portfolio perform? What's the roadmap?"
**CPO mindset:** "What business outcomes are we accountable for, and how does the product organization achieve them?"

Practical consequences:

- **Language changes deliberately.** Drop product vocabulary (features, roadmaps, sprints) in executive forums; switch to business language (ROI, revenue, retention, margin). Reserve product vocabulary for the product team.
- **Your primary customer may shift.** At a company pursuing an exit or growth financing, the CPO's primary customer may be the investor or acquirer, not the end user. The persona depends on business context.
- **Your team shifts.** As CPO, your most important team is not the product org — it's the executive staff. Those relationships determine whether the product org gets what it needs.

### Time horizon expansion

| Level | Short-term | Long-term |
|---|---|---|
| IC | Sprint | Quarter |
| Director | Quarter | 1–2 years |
| VP | 1–2 quarters | 3 years |
| CPO | 1–2 quarters | 3–5 years |

The long horizon doesn't replace short-term accountability — it runs in parallel. You still deliver every quarter. "Sweeping up after the elephants" remains part of the job; you're just also responsible for knowing where the parade is going in three years.

### Alliance building

At VP/CPO level the executive team is your primary operating environment, and alliances aren't optional — without them you're a dead man walking. Executives can appear supportive in the meeting and undermine the decision outside it. This means: weekly engagement with peer executives, proactive trade-off communication ("you're not getting X this quarter because of Y, and here's why that's right for the business"), bringing people along *before* announcements, and understanding each peer's real priorities rather than their stated ones.

**Pitfalls:** conflating the VP and CPO transitions (they're different jobs); waiting to be empowered; neglecting the alliance layer; misidentifying which of the four situations you're actually in.

## Executive onboarding: the first 90 days

The most common failure in senior product transitions is **acting before understanding** — changing structures, replacing people, or announcing strategy before building the evidence base that makes those decisions defensible.

### The consultant mindset

Enter as if you were an external consultant hired to assess the organization, before you're the person responsible for changing it.

- **Observe before diagnosing.** Don't form opinions in week one from first impressions.
- **Ask before declaring.** "Help me understand how this works" beats "here's what we're doing differently."
- **Understand how the steering connects to the rudder.** Systems and relationships look one way on paper and work completely differently in practice. Map that reality first.
- **Don't throw the big red switch.** You wouldn't throw an unlabeled switch in a power plant you'd never operated. Same logic for inherited org structures, processes, reporting lines, and team compositions.

**Negotiate this upfront.** Tell your boss and peers that month one is explicitly a learning phase and your first major recommendations land in month two. Executives who've been through transitions respect it; executives who want action in week one are themselves a signal.

### Three phases

**Phase 1 — Diagnose (month 1).** Listen tours across product, engineering, sales, support, and customers. Find the **unwritten strategy** — what the organization actually optimizes for, which is rarely what the strategy deck says. Assess people in two categories: capability in the current role, and capacity for where the org is going.

**Phase 2 — Validate (month 2).** Test your month-one hypotheses. Build the **body of evidence** — the documented basis for the changes you're about to propose. Socialize findings before recommending, so nobody is surprised in the room.

**Phase 3 — Act with evidence (month 3).** Now make the structural calls, with the evidence attached. Changes made this way survive the first challenge; changes made in week two do not.

**Pitfalls:** performing action instead of building evidence; staying in consultant mode past month two (at some point observation becomes avoidance); trusting the loudest voice; skipping the CEO interview *before accepting the role* — the single highest-leverage diagnostic available, and it's only available before you say yes; ignoring executive dysfunction on the theory that you'll fix it from inside.

## Product-sense interviews

For "how would you improve X?", "design a product for Y", "what would you build next?"

### The six-part answer spine

1. **Clarify** — reduce ambiguity, define scope, state assumptions
2. **Rationale** — why this problem matters now, for the market and the company
3. **Product goal** — the user outcome you want, before any feature talk
4. **Segmentation** — choose who to serve first, and show why that target wins
5. **Pain points** — map the journey, name the frictions, pick the one worth solving first
6. **Solution** — generate distinct options, compare, commit to one MVP with explicit exclusions

**Order matters.** Jump from prompt to feature ideas and the answer sounds clever but ungrounded. Establish user, goal, and pain first and the solution feels earned.

**What's actually being tested:** can you clarify ambiguity without freezing; tie user value to business logic; segment instead of saying "everyone"; prioritize one pain instead of describing ten; make tradeoffs explicit; communicate clearly under time pressure.

**Delivery matters as much as content** — this is a *spoken* answer. Signpost each section out loud ("let me start by clarifying scope"), keep each section short, and stop talking when you've committed to the MVP.

**Pitfalls:** solution-first thinking; segmentation theater (naming segments then ignoring them); a goal that's really a feature; "pain points" that are actually missing features; three fake options where two exist to be rejected; a weak close with no commitment; over-answering every branch instead of choosing.
