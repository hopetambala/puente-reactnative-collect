# Strategy and Positioning

Who you serve, what you're not, where growth comes from, and how you say it. These play badly out of order — positioning before you know the problem is guessing, and a strategy session before positioning is a workshop about nothing.

---

## Positioning statement (component)

Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm*. Two halves.

**Value proposition:**
```
For        [target customer]
that need  [underserved need]
[product name]
is a       [product category]
that       [benefit statement]
```

**Differentiation statement:**
```
Unlike     [primary competitor or competitive alternative]
[product name]
provides   [unique differentiation]
```

**Why this structure works.** It forbids "for everyone." It exposes assumptions — if you can't complete "Unlike X," you may not have defensible differentiation, and finding that out on one line beats finding it out after a launch. It forces outcomes over features ("that cuts month-end close from three days to three hours" beats "that has analytics"). And the category choice quietly determines how buyers evaluate you: calling yourself a CRM invites a CRM feature comparison you may lose.

**The competitive alternative is often not a competitor.** For a lot of products it's a spreadsheet, a manual process, or doing nothing. Naming a real rival when the actual alternative is inertia produces messaging that misses.

**Stress test:** could a competitor make the same claim? If yes, it isn't differentiation. Can you prove the "provides"? If not, it's aspiration.

**Pitfalls:** "for everyone" (positioning theater); a feature list in the benefit slot; an imaginary competitor chosen because they're easy to beat; differentiation with no proof; category confusion that puts you in an evaluation you can't win.

## Positioning workshop (interactive)

Five questions under the facilitation protocol, producing the statement above:

1. **Target customer segment** — who first? Push past "SMBs" to a describable buyer.
2. **Underserved need** — in JTBD terms, not feature terms.
3. **Product category** — what shelf are you on? What do they compare you to?
4. **Key benefit** — outcome, not capability. What's measurably different in their week?
5. **Competitive differentiation** — versus the real alternative, including doing nothing.

Output the value prop, the differentiation statement, and a one-sentence plain-language version someone can say out loud without reading.

**Pitfalls:** the need turning out to be a feature request; differentiation that's a feature ("we have SSO") rather than a stance; no customer validation behind any of it.

## Press release — working backwards (component)

Amazon's forcing function: write the launch announcement *before* building, and if it isn't compelling, don't build it.

**Structure:**
1. **Headline** — clear, benefit-focused
2. **Dateline** — city, date
3. **Introduction** — what launched, who it's for, the key benefit
4. **Problem** — the customer problem, in their words
5. **Solution** — how it addresses the problem, in outcomes
6. **Leader quote** — the vision and the customer commitment
7. **Supporting details** — additional benefits or data
8. **Boilerplate** — company background
9. **Call to action** — how to get it
10. **Media contact**

**The test:** would a customer reading this care? Would they change what they do? If the excitement lives entirely in paragraph 7's feature list, the value proposition isn't there yet.

**Pitfalls:** a feature list instead of benefits; a vague problem paragraph ("customers want more flexibility"); jargon; a generic executive quote that could belong to any company ("we're excited to continue our journey"); no data or validation anywhere.

## Product strategy session (workflow, 2–4 weeks)

The full arc, with decision points that let you exit early.

- **Phase 1 — Positioning & market context** (days 1–2): positioning workshop, competitive landscape.
  → *Do we have enough customer context?* If no, go do discovery first.
- **Phase 2 — Problem framing & validation** (days 3–5): problem statements, interviews, evidence.
  → *Is the problem validated?* An unvalidated problem doesn't get a roadmap slot.
- **Phase 3 — Solution exploration** (week 2, days 1–3): Opportunity Solution Tree, solution options.
  → *Do we need to test before committing?* Usually yes.
- **Phase 4 — Prioritization & roadmap** (week 2, days 4–5).
- **Phase 5 — Stakeholder alignment & communication** (week 3).
- **Phase 6 — Execution planning** (week 4).

**Pitfalls:** skipping validation because the deadline is real; running it as a solo PM exercise and calling the readout "alignment"; no executive sponsor, so nothing that comes out of it can be funded; running all phases regardless of the decision points; the session quietly becoming a permanent process.

## Recommendation canvas (component)

Built for higher-uncertainty bets — AI features especially. Ten components in one defensible view:

1. **Business outcome** — what's in it for the business
2. **Product outcome** — what's in it for the customer
3. **Problem statement** — persona-centric framing
4. **Solution hypothesis** — if/then, with tiny acts of discovery and a proof-of-life probe
5. **Positioning statement** — value prop + differentiation
6. **Assumptions & unknowns** — what could invalidate this
7. **PESTEL risks** — split into *investigate now* and *monitor*
8. **Value justification** — why it's worth doing
9. **Success metrics** — SMART, with a "we'd act differently if this moved" clause
10. **What's next** — named next steps with owners

**Pitfalls:** vague outcomes; solution-first thinking dressed as a canvas; skipping the discovery experiments; PESTEL risks so generic they apply to any company ("regulation may change"); value justification that's just enthusiasm.

## Growth options: the Ansoff Matrix (workflow)

Four quadrants, one question: *where does the next tranche of growth come from, and at what risk?* This is a research instrument, not whiteboarding — every candidate answers **"what documented signal says this demand exists?"**

| | Existing market | New market |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing product** | **Market penetration** — lowest risk | **Market development** |
| **New product** | **Product development** | **Diversification** — highest risk |

Close with a **recommended sequence**, not a list. Growth options compound: penetration funds development. And state the risk gradient explicitly — teams that present four quadrants as four equal options are hiding the gradient that makes the matrix useful.

**Pitfalls:** the brainstorm grid (entries with no evidence); risk-gradient denial; quadrant stuffing (padding thin quadrants for symmetry); options without a sequence; sizing by vibe.

## McKinsey growth paths (interactive triage)

Faster than Ansoff when the question is "where's our constraint?" Two axes:

- **Customer/market context:** Known ↔ Less known
- **Degree of product change:** Low ↔ High

| | Low product change | High product change |
|---|---|---|
| **Known context** | **L2 — New customer segments** | **L4 — New distribution channels** |
| **Less-known context** | **L3 — New geographies** | **L5 — New products** |

**Order matters.** Risk scales with distance from the core. Most teams overinvest in L5 before exhausting L2 and L4. If core product-market fit is strong and the current market isn't saturated, L2 or L4 is almost always the right first move.

**The knowledge principle:** innovation at any level is downstream of accumulated contextual knowledge. Teams that win at L3 aren't the fastest movers — they're the ones who studied how value travels in the new market before scaling. Teams that win at L5 built the adjacent thing existing customers were already asking for, in language those customers already used.

**Pitfalls:** L5 before L2/L4 is exhausted; confusing a marketing campaign with a distribution channel; geographic expansion before you understand local word of mouth; treating adjacent segments as the same ICP; using the matrix as a precision instrument rather than a lane-picker.

## SWOT (workflow, evidence-cited)

Not a four-box brainstorm. Strengths and weaknesses come from documented capability and customer voice; opportunities and threats from market, competitor, and macro signals. Every entry carries a source and a Fact/Inference/Assumption label.

**The part that makes it worth doing is the crossings:**
- **S-O** — which strength lets us capture which opportunity?
- **W-T** — which weakness exposes us to which threat?

A SWOT without crossings is a parking lot with four sections.

**Pitfalls:** the four-box brainstorm; opportunities that are actually strategies ("expand into Europe" is a plan, not a market condition); strengths sourced from your own marketing page; skipping the crossings; wish-list contamination (things you want to be true).

## Porter's Five Forces (workflow)

Each force rated weak/moderate/strong **with the documented signals that justify the rating** — a rating without signals is vibes.

1. **Competitive rivalry**
2. **Threat of new entrants**
3. **Threat of substitutes** — *name the AI substitute explicitly.* In most categories right now, the most credible substitute is "a competent team with a model and a weekend," and analyses that omit it are already stale.
4. **Buyer power**
5. **Supplier power**

**Close at the profit pool:** where does the margin sit, and who is squeezing it? Five Forces is an argument about where money goes, not a diagram for slide four.

**Pitfalls:** ratings as vibes; skipping the AI substitute; confusing named competitors with structural forces; no profit-pool close; forcing structure onto a category too nascent to have any.

## PESTEL (component) and PESTEL delta monitor (workflow)

**PESTEL** scans macro forces you don't control: **P**olitical, **E**conomic, **S**ocial, **T**echnological, **E**nvironmental, **L**egal. Per factor: what's happening, the source, the impact on this product, and the timeframe. Synthesize into top opportunities, top threats, and recommendations. Skip nothing — the factor you assume is irrelevant is usually the one that bites (accessibility law, data residency, model-provider terms).

**PESTEL delta monitor** turns it from a workshop artifact into a radar. Quarterly, diff each factor against the prior run and report **material movement only**. The real output is the **broken assumptions** section — the baseline entries that were live assumptions rather than furniture, and have now been falsified.

**Pitfalls:** generic analysis that would fit any company; ignoring low-impact factors entirely rather than noting them as unmoved; no sources; analysis with no action; treating it as a one-time exercise; on re-scans, re-debating the twenty factors that didn't move instead of examining the two that did; diffing across a pivot, where the baseline no longer describes the same business.
